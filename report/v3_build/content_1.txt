= CHAPTER 1 | INTRODUCTION
== 1.1 Coffee Cultivation in Karnataka
Nearly 70% of the coffee grown in India is produced in Karnataka. Most of it comes from small family farms scattered across Kodagu, Chikmagalur and Hassan, and for these families coffee is not a sideline but the primary crop. If leaf rust or leaf miner takes hold in a season, the family may have a poor year.
In such farms, much knowledge concerning the daily workings of the farm is passed on by word of mouth: one neighbour recalls similar leaf patches appearing the previous year; one relative is enthusiastic about a certain spray and another about a particular fertiliser; the estate manager has an idea of what the weather will do. This experience is useful, but it is inconsistent and unrecorded. It is also not accessible when the closest extension officer is located two valleys away and an infection is already under way.
== 1.2 About PlantIQ
PlantIQ started from a simple question. What would it take for a grower to point a phone at a sick leaf, receive actionable advice in the language they speak at home, and then use the same phone to offer the harvest to a buyer? That question cannot be answered by an image classifier alone. A label such as "Leaf Rust" says little until it is tied to this week's weather, a suitable treatment, a safe quantity and, eventually, a buyer.
The problem has been divided into four modules, summarised in Table 1.1. Perception is handled by a computer-vision model, advice by a retrieval-augmented engine, conversation by a Kannada and English chatbot, and the market by a direct listing and discovery module.

== 1.3 Progress Up to Phase 2
Phase 1 covered identifying the scope, reading the literature, verifying access to the tools and APIs, and checking whether the data was available. During Phase 2 every module was constructed by a different team member and was tested individually. By the end of that period, the classifier could process an image through a FastAPI endpoint, structured advisories were produced by the retrieval pipeline with simulated weather, listings were stored and mapped in the marketplace, and the chatbot was basic, with English voice input. The modules were not communicating with each other, the speech recognition system achieved only 55% word accuracy on Kannada words in the disease vocabulary, the weather values were simulated and no actual users had used the system. Phase 3 was also expected to be the most critical and high-risk phase, because of integration.
== 1.4 Scope of Phase 3
The only goal of Phase 3 was to convert four individual prototypes into one product that functions from start to finish and is testable. This in practice was equivalent to going back through almost every module and not just wiring them together. === 1.5.1 Local Language Integration
Greater emphasis on making Kannada an integral part of the product, rather than an add-on, was requested by the reviewers. The earlier plan was to translate English advice into Kannada and to take voice input through a browser speech API that was not very good with disease vocabulary. Both have since been changed. The first choice for speech is now a local speech model, with cloud fallbacks behind it; Kannada text is handled by having the language model answer directly in Kannada script, so that no translation step sits between the advice and the grower.
=== 1.5.2 A Standard Input for RAG and CNN
During Phase 2, the two modules had slightly different conceptions of what a request is, and their combination revealed minor differences. One structured input was agreed upon, which includes the disease, the confidence, the location and the user's question. Both modules read a scan in the same way, so a scan returns the same answer whether it is viewed on the scan screen or in the chat.
=== 1.5.3 Testing with People Who Know the Field
The first field testing was conducted and feedback was received from the planters; their comments on usability were positive. It was not a formal trial but an initial round, and no statistics on task completion were recorded. A structured pilot with a larger group is still planned.
=== 1.5.4 Testing the Newly Trained CNN
The newly trained CNN was tested: the classifier was retrained and a testing pipeline was created. It achieved 90% accuracy when tested on the validation set and 80% accuracy when tested on new images of leaves that were not included in training or validation. The ten-point gap is a valuable, realistic measure: the model is better on images that are similar to its training set than on new images. This is discussed in Section 8.2, and it is used to justify the 75% confidence gate. A later leak-free five-fold evaluation on the public JMuBEN images, also reported in Section 8.2, measured an accuracy of 97.9%.
=== 1.5.5 Streamlining the Integration Flow
The final suggestion was to streamline the end-to-end integration flow. All four modules now sit behind a single backend with which the mobile app communicates, and the entire workflow from sign-in to scan, advice, chat and listing was tested manually and through integration tests. Making this happen took longer than the wiring itself. A shared session was added, which made it possible for the chatbot to see the last scan of the user. Bugs that occurred only when modules called each other were identified and corrected, for example a cache key that did not contain the query.

= CHAPTER 2 | PROBLEM STATEMENT
The growers for whom PlantIQ is built are located where four separate problems meet. Each one is well known on its own, but they are usually treated as if they had nothing to do with one another. In the field they all arrive at the same time.
== 2.1 Scattered Cultivation Knowledge
Dependable information on sowing, irrigation, pest control and harvesting does exist, but it is spread across institute bulletins, board guidelines, research papers, videos and conversations. Much of it is dated or written for another region. Material that ignores Karnataka's crops, cultivators and climate is of little use to a grower deciding what to spray this week, and the usual outcome is guesswork or paying a middleman for advice that may itself be second-hand.
== 2.2 The Gap Between Diagnosis and Action
Plant-disease classifiers are now common and several are accurate under laboratory conditions. Most of them stop at the label. A farmer told "Leaf Rust, 91%" still has to work out how serious that is, whether the coming weather will make it worse, what to apply and at what concentration. A label with no next step leaves most of the work undone.
== 2.3 Trust and Language in AI Advice
Large language models write fluent agronomic advice, but fluency is not the same as correctness. A model can state a fungicide dose with full confidence and be wrong, and in farming a wrong dose wastes money, damages the crop or harms the person spraying. An advisory tool for growers therefore has to be tied to a trusted source and has to show when it has stepped outside it.
Language adds to the difficulty. Many growers are more at ease in Kannada than in English, and typing long technical sentences on a phone is a barrier of its own. A tool that expects English text quietly shuts out the people who need it most.
== 2.4 Limited Market Visibility
Even a good crop is hard to put in front of buyers. Growers frequently have to rely on middlemen, reducing their cut of the price and their bargaining power. It is difficult to contact roasters, traders and specialty buyers directly, and many growers never see offers from other buyers.
== 2.5 Formal Problem Statement
Taking all of these together, the problem addressed in this work is:
Q: Coffee growers in Karnataka lack a single localised, language-aware platform on which they can identify a leaf problem, receive advice grounded in trusted sources and current weather, ask follow-up questions in Kannada, and offer their crop directly to buyers.
== 2.6 Scope of the Work
The work reported here covers coffee leaf-disease advice and a direct marketplace. Coffee was chosen because the disease set is small enough to cover properly, the region is well defined, and the Central Coffee Research Institute (CCRI) documents provide an authoritative text source in which to ground the advice.

= CHAPTER 3 | LITERATURE REVIEW
Seven papers were read in Phases 1 and 2. Each covers a component of what PlantIQ aims to assemble: disease detection, evidence-based advice, structured knowledge, multimodal models, farmer-facing chat and the role of soil and climate. The summaries are paraphrased, and all figures are those reported by the authors.
== 3.1 RAG for Coffee Leaf Disease Remediation [1]
=== 3.1.1 Abstract
In this research, an intelligent framework for the detection and remediation of coffee leaf diseases using computer vision and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) has been proposed. A YOLOv8 model is used by the system to detect the disease in each image of a coffee leaf, and FAISS-based semantic retrieval is then used to obtain information from an agricultural knowledge corpus. The passages are fed into prompts provided to GPT-3.5 to produce treatment recommendations specific to the disease, based on agricultural references. The proposed approach addresses the tendency of standalone language models to provide unsupported agricultural advice. The experimental results show that visual disease recognition with knowledge-grounded advisory generation can achieve about 12% accuracy improvement over a language-model-only baseline and reduce the number of hallucinated statements by 30%, indicating the effectiveness of the combination.
=== 3.1.2 Research Gap
Traditional disease detection systems primarily aim to diagnose diseases, but do not offer useful recommendations for treatment. Current methods fail to consider factors such as real-time environmental conditions, chemical dose verification and regional language accessibility when providing generated advice, even though RAG enhances the factual basis of the advice. Additionally, a platform that integrates disease diagnosis, personalised agricultural recommendations and direct market access has not been investigated.
=== 3.1.3 Advantages
The proposed framework is able to seamlessly combine visual disease detection with knowledge-based advisory generation, mitigating the reliance on unsupported language-model answers. The semantic retrieval with FAISS enhances the contextual relevance of treatment recommendations, and the modular design enables independent enhancements in detection and generation. The 12% accuracy boost and 30% decrease in hallucinations highlight the promise of RAG in creating more accurate agricultural advisory services.
=== 3.1.4 Disadvantages
The system does not include real-time weather data such as humidity, precipitation and temperature in the treatment recommendation. It also does not include specific reference to the doses and amounts of pesticides and chemicals given in official agricultural texts. Moreover, the lack of regional-language support and direct marketplace integration reduces its usability and accessibility as a comprehensive farmer-support platform.
== 3.2 Language Models with Agricultural Knowledge Graphs [2]
=== 3.2.1 Abstract
To enhance information retrieval and explainability, Zhao et al. present an agricultural advisory framework that integrates large language models (LLMs) with structured knowledge graphs. The system builds a Neo4j knowledge graph of the relationships between crop, pathogen, soil condition and disease symptom. The extracted structural representation is processed by GraphSAGE and Graph Attention Networks, and the textual information is processed by a BERT-based encoder. To produce contextually relevant answers from agricultural subgraphs, these representations are coupled with GPT-Neo. The framework achieves about 94% precision and 92% recall, showing the strong performance of the combination of structured agricultural relations and language generation. The approach also makes the results more interpretable by allowing the exploration of the relationships between symptoms, diseases and treatments via the knowledge graph.
=== 3.2.2 Research Gap
Traditional systems for document retrieval often have difficulty capturing complex relationships among the various factors involved in the agricultural context, including soil properties, disease symptoms, crop varieties and treatments. Knowledge graphs overcome this by being structured and supporting reasoning, but they require considerable domain expertise and computing power to build and maintain. Further research is needed to incorporate graph-based reasoning into real-time disease detection, environmental monitoring and region-specific agricultural advice.
=== 3.2.3 Advantages
The proposed framework offers a structured representation of agricultural knowledge to enable more explainable and contextually appropriate recommendations. It can perform reasoning on interrelated agricultural entities with the help of the combination of GraphSAGE, Graph Attention Networks, BERT and GPT-Neo. The reported 94% precision and 92% recall are good results, and the framework is able to extract the links between symptoms and treatments, which is more transparent than traditional text-based retrieval methods.
=== 3.2.4 Disadvantages
Extensive data preparation, domain knowledge and computational resources are needed to develop and maintain a comprehensive agricultural knowledge graph. The architecture adds to the complexity of traditional semantic retrieval systems and may need regular updates as agricultural knowledge continues to develop. Moreover, the proposed framework is not designed specifically for coffee growing in Karnataka and lacks integration of image-based disease diagnosis, real-time weather data and direct coffee marketplace functionality.
== 3.3 Coffee Leaf Disease Detection with a CNN [3]
=== 3.3.1 Abstract
The aim of this research is to implement Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for the automatic classification of diseased coffee leaves. This research employs around 8,500 images depicting coffee leaf rust, Cercospora and leaf miner conditions. To increase the training diversity, image augmentation methods are used, and a CNN similar to GoogLeNet is trained using the Adam optimiser and a cross-entropy loss function. The network automatically extracts visual features related to disease symptoms, reducing the reliance on manually designed image descriptors. The experimental results show a classification accuracy of about 90%, which is better than the Support Vector Machine and the smaller neural network baselines that were evaluated. The results confirm the efficiency of deep convolutional networks in detecting common coffee leaf diseases through image analysis.
=== 3.3.2 Research Gap
Coffee disease diagnosis is usually done manually and requires agricultural skills, which can cause delays. CNN-based systems automate this process, but they are not necessarily able to cope with uncontrolled field images with variations in lighting, background, leaf orientation and disease severity. Further, classification-based approaches tend to focus on the classification alone, do not incorporate any confidence-based decision-making and do not relate disease predictions to treatment options or to an assessment of environmental risk.
=== 3.3.3 Advantages
The proposed CNN shows a good classification capability, with a mean accuracy of about 90% across three classes of coffee leaf diseases. Image augmentation increases the diversity of the training data, and automatic feature extraction saves manual image processing. Its better performance compared with standard machine-learning models validates CNNs as a worthwhile tool for automatic coffee disease recognition and for broader agricultural applications.
=== 3.3.4 Disadvantages
The model covers only three types of disease, which means that other common conditions of coffee leaves are not covered. The reported results are not sufficiently robust in terms of performance on uncontrolled field photographs to be certain of their reliability in the real world. Moreover, the system has no mechanism for rejecting uncertain predictions, no environmental context and no generation of treatment advice, all of which are required for a system that goes beyond disease classification.
== 3.4 Multimodal Language Models in Agriculture [4]
=== 3.4.1 Abstract
A thorough review of over 200 research papers on the use of large language models in agriculture was conducted by Sapkota and co-authors. The study compares model families such as GPT-4, Gemini, LLaMA, PaLM and DeepSeek, as well as approaches such as vision-language integration, chain-of-thought prompting and retrieval-augmented generation. Special focus is given to the integration of agricultural imagery, textual information and environmental measurements for better decision-making. The examined results show that multimodal retrieval-based methods can increase the accuracy by about 10-25% compared with single-modal retrieval-based methods in the tested scenarios. The authors also point out the significant computational needs of training large models from scratch and propose pretrained architectures with targeted fine-tuning as a more practical way to develop such systems.
=== 3.4.2 Research Gap
Existing agricultural AI systems usually process visual, textual and environmental data separately, which prevents them from making comprehensive recommendations. While multimodal models provide more context, there are still issues with computation speed, model accuracy, data integration and deployment in low-resource settings. There remains a lack of adequate evidence in the literature of the integration of multimodal disease diagnosis with verified advisory generation and a farmer-oriented service in a region-specific agricultural system.
=== 3.4.3 Advantages
The review covers the research on agricultural language models in depth and emphasises the benefits of using multiple information modalities. Multimodal retrieval approaches show promising improvements in accuracy, reported as around 10-25%. The research also shows that pretrained models, targeted fine-tuning and retrieval augmentation are viable approaches to reduce computational expense and enhance the contextual relevance of agricultural AI models.
=== 3.4.4 Disadvantages
The study is a literature review and does not present an end-to-end agricultural advisory platform developed by the authors. The reported performance gains come from different research contexts, so direct comparison is difficult. Furthermore, multimodal systems face computational, data alignment, network dependency and deployment challenges. A review cannot by itself validate disease detection, multilingual interaction and marketplace integration specific to the state of Karnataka.
== 3.5 Farmer.Chat [5]
=== 3.5.1 Abstract
Farmer.Chat is a conversational agricultural advisory platform designed to increase the accessibility of farming information for smallholder farmers. It works on widely used apps such as WhatsApp and Telegram, so users do not have to learn anything complicated. It utilises ElasticSearch document retrieval and BERT embeddings to locate relevant agricultural information, and uses a fine-tuned GPT-based language model to generate a conversational response. The authors report serving over 15,000 farmers and processing about 300,000 queries. About 75% of the advice was judged accurate by agronomists, and farmers' comments focused on the positive aspects of conversational engagement and language flexibility. The study provides a glimpse into the viability and potential of agricultural chatbots that are accessible and easy to use.
=== 3.5.2 Research Gap
Knowledge about agriculture is frequently spread over technical literature and institutional sources that are not easily accessible to small-scale farmers. Farmer.Chat addresses this problem of accessibility by using familiar messaging systems and natural-language interaction. However, current conversational advisory systems are not yet capable of combining image-based disease diagnosis, location-specific environmental data and domain-specific knowledge about coffee cultivation. The link between agricultural advisory services and direct involvement in markets is also weak.
=== 3.5.3 Advantages
The platform is designed to be accessible and to interact conversationally through popular messaging apps. Its retrieval-based architecture helps to deliver relevant agricultural information, and its language flexibility makes it more user-friendly for a variety of farming communities. The reported adoption by more than 15,000 farmers and some 300,000 queries indicates that the application is effective. Evaluation by agronomists is also helpful in assessing the reliability of the generated recommendations.
=== 3.5.4 Disadvantages
About 75% of the generated advice is deemed accurate, so there is room for improvement in the reliability and verification of the advice. The platform lacks integrated image-based coffee disease detection and a knowledge base targeting coffee diseases in the coffee-growing regions of Karnataka. Besides that, the framework does not include explicit chemical dosage validation, live microclimate integration or direct farmer-to-buyer marketplace functionality, which limits it to conversational agricultural assistance.
== 3.6 Ensemble Models for Coffee Yield Prediction [6]
=== 3.6.1 Abstract
Santhosha and Umesh examine the use of ensemble machine-learning algorithms to predict coffee yield from soil and agricultural data. The study uses about 19,000 soil records collected from the Central Coffee Research Institute (CCRI), Karnataka, to study the relation between soil characteristics and coffee productivity. The predictive performance of supervised learning algorithms such as Random Forest, Decision Tree, Gradient Boosting and Extra Trees is compared. According to the experimental results, Random Forest and Extra Trees perform best, with results of about 91% being reported. The research also shows that the yield of coffee differs significantly with even a slight change in soil chemistry. The findings show the potential of ensemble learning to predict agricultural productivity and to plan cultivation based on data.
=== 3.6.2 Research Gap
The productivity of coffee is related to a number of interacting factors, including soil composition, nutrient availability, environmental factors and disease incidence. Yield estimation methods used in traditional agriculture are not necessarily able to capture the nonlinear relationships between these parameters. Ensemble models are useful in improving soil-based yield prediction, but are not directly useful for disease identification, treatment recommendations or real-time environmental advisory requirements. There is still great potential to integrate soil-based predictive information into larger systems of agricultural intelligence.
=== 3.6.3 Advantages
The study is based on a large database of about 19,000 soil records linked to CCRI, which can provide relevant information for coffee cultivation in Karnataka. By comparing several machine learning algorithms, the study identifies which algorithms are effective for prediction, with Random Forest and Extra Trees performing at around 91%. The study shows the importance of soil chemistry in coffee productivity and provides a basis for the inclusion of soil-health analysis in agricultural decision-support systems.
=== 3.6.4 Disadvantages
The research is mainly on predicting yields and not on the diagnosis of disease or the recommendation of treatment. The predictive performance relies on the quality and representativeness of the soil records, and the approach described does not continuously integrate live environmental measurements. Moreover, the system lacks the ability to recognise disease from images, to provide advisory services through conversation and to offer any agricultural market functionality, providing only the capacity to estimate productivity.
== 3.7 Elevation and Rainfall Effects on Coffee [7]
=== 3.7.1 Abstract
Patil et al. explore the effect of elevation and rainfall on coffee plant attributes with a study of cultivation plots in the Chikmagalur and Hassan districts of Karnataka. Measurable plant characteristics such as leaf area, leaf weight, node density and bean-related characteristics were explored to find relationships between environmental factors and coffee development. Statistical analysis reports a significant positive correlation between specific leaf area and elevation (r = 0.912). There is a moderate correlation of r = 0.475 between rainfall and bean defects. These results show that the physiological and crop responses of the coffee plant change measurably with differences in local environmental conditions, emphasising the need to consider geographical and climatic context in agricultural research and advisory development.
=== 3.7.2 Research Gap
Agricultural advisory systems often give generic advice, neglecting local differences in elevation, rainfall and microclimate. The study establishes statistical relationships between the environment and coffee characteristics, but the results are not converted into an automated decision-support tool. Combining environmental information from mobile sources with disease recognition and treatment recommendations is an interesting area for research, offering the prospect of more context-aware agricultural advisory systems.
=== 3.7.3 Advantages
The findings of this research are geographically relevant to coffee cultivation in Karnataka, as the study uses field-based data from two districts, Chikmagalur and Hassan. The high correlation between elevation and specific leaf area (r = 0.912) and the moderate correlation between rainfall and bean defects (r = 0.475) indicate the importance of local adaptation. The results offer empirical evidence of the need to integrate location and weather data into agricultural monitoring and advisory services.
=== 3.7.4 Disadvantages
The study does not focus on the development of an operational agricultural decision-support system, but rather on statistical relationships. Correlation does not prove causation, and results from the surveyed areas might need to be validated in other growing areas. Moreover, the research does not include automation for disease detection, real-time environmental monitoring, recommendation generation or interaction with farmers in multiple languages, which restricts its immediate use in an integrated agricultural advisory system.

== 3.9 Research Gap
Every ingredient used in PlantIQ is supported by the literature. CNNs classify coffee leaves, retrieval cuts down invented advice, chatbots reach smallholders through messaging apps, and climate clearly affects the behaviour of coffee plants. However, no work was found that combines all of these for the coffee growers of Karnataka, and this is where the gaps remain. Diagnosis stops at the label: existing detectors name the disease but do not tie it to the local humidity and temperature or provide a suitable remedy, which is left to the reader. Advice is not verified: retrieval reduces hallucination, but none of the identified systems checks the numbers in its own response. A simple and useful safety measure is to check whether each chemical dose in the answer appears in the source text, and none of the examined works does this.
Language support is available but limited. Most agricultural AI tools accept only English text, and Farmer.Chat is the main exception. No system offered a coffee-specific chain from voice input to a reply in Kannada script with the grower's own scan as context. Regional focus is rare: the retrieval systems cover general content or other crops and areas, and none is based on the CCRI documents, the disease set of Karnataka or Arabica estates at altitude. Markets are disconnected: agricultural marketplaces exist, but they are not linked to advisory tools, so a grower who has just dealt with a leaf problem and wants to sell has to change apps and contacts. Field readiness is seldom discussed: many papers report accuracy on a curated test set with very little discussion of response time, repeat requests, failing third-party APIs or security, which matter as much as accuracy in a tool that gives spray advice and holds user accounts. The PlantIQ pipeline covers all of these in one app: a leaf is photographed, grounded advice based on the weather is received, follow-up questions are asked in the grower's own words, and the crop is listed for sale.
